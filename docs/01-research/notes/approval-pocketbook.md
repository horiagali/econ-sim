# Approval and own finances — what the evidence says about sizes and signs

## Question
For the first version of [approval](../../02-design/society/opinion-approval.md) (roadmap M5, own finances only, display only): which personal economic conditions move a person's view of the government, in which direction, and roughly how much relative to each other?

## Short answer
- **Economic voting is robust:** incumbent support rises with income growth and falls with unemployment and inflation (review: Lewis-Beck and Stegmaier 2000, 2013). Confidence high for the signs, medium for sizes.
- **National conditions matter more than personal ones for votes (sociotropic over pocketbook)** in most studies, but the personal effect is real and larger for people directly hit (job loss, falling income). The first version uses personal conditions only, as the roadmap asks; the national component comes in M14. Confidence medium.
- **Losses weigh about twice gains** (loss aversion, Kahneman and Tversky 1979; negativity bias in economic voting, Soroka 2006). Confidence medium.
- **Unemployment hurts more than inflation, point for point:** in life-satisfaction data a point of unemployment costs about 1.7 points of inflation (Di Tella, MacCulloch and Oswald 2001); being unemployed oneself is one of the largest falls in well-being found (Clark and Oswald 1994). Inflation is disliked even when incomes keep up (Shiller 1997). Confidence medium.
- **Reference points move:** people judge a change against what they have been used to for a year or two; approval responds within months (Lewis-Beck and Stegmaier). Confidence low for the exact speeds.

## Findings
1. **Signs and relative weights** (confidence medium). The economic-voting literature finds a percentage point of real income growth worth roughly one to two points of incumbent vote share, and unemployment and inflation each costing a similar or somewhat larger amount per point; estimates vary greatly by country and period.
2. **Personal unemployment** (confidence medium). The unemployed and their households report much lower approval of incumbents and life satisfaction; the effect on others in the household is smaller but present.
3. **Inflation felt** (confidence medium). Perceived inflation follows the prices of frequently bought goods (food, fuel, energy), so poorer households, whose baskets weigh those more, feel more of it. In the game's first version every household has the same basket (households, M2), so felt inflation is the CPI for all.
4. **Hardship** (confidence low). Inability to afford basic needs is strongly associated with distrust in government in European surveys (Eurobarometer, EU-SILC ad hoc modules); no size usable directly.
5. **Romania** (confidence low). Trust in the president and government in 2021 was low (Eurobarometer trust in national government about 20–30%), so a national average near 50 in the first version is a scale, not a calibrated level.

## Numbers worth keeping

| Quantity | Value | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion factor | about 2 | Kahneman and Tversky 1979; Soroka 2006 | medium |
| Unemployment against inflation, per point | about 1.7 | Di Tella, MacCulloch and Oswald 2001 | medium |
| Vote share per point of real income growth | about 1–2 points | Lewis-Beck and Stegmaier 2000 | medium |

## Implications for the game
The first version of [opinion and approval](../../02-design/society/opinion-approval.md) has four components (real income change against a reference, unemployment of self and household, inflation, hardship) with weights in this order of size and losses counted twice. The weights are guesses to tune in M14 against polls.
